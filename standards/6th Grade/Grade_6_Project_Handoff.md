## Recovered learning assignments and dependencies

### Mathematics

The progression is **quantitative relationships → symbolic and spatial models → geometric design → evaluation of data claims**. Number fluency supports ratio reasoning and later formulas. Factoring precedes equivalent expressions. Signed number order precedes coordinate distance and inequalities. Coordinates and expressions then support geometry. The final project makes variability and statistical reasoning explicit.

#### S4 — A Better Park: Report and Presentation

**Student challenge:** What is one change that would make a neighborhood park better for people and the environment? Investigate a problem, compare possible solutions, and present your recommendation to an audience considering park improvements.

**Project type:** Research report and presentation. Use a familiar park with accessible information or a teacher-provided park case containing photos, a map and clearly identified environmental data. Students learning online can use the provided case without visiting a site. The audience may be classmates acting as reviewers; an actual public presentation is optional.

**What students do:**

1. Choose one bounded problem, such as excessive water use, runoff from a path, loss of wildlife habitat or wasteful outdoor lighting. Explain who or what is affected and identify the scientific question to investigate.
2. Research the problem using a small set of credible, accessible sources. Gather relevant facts, observations and data, and explain the scientific cause-and-effect relationships in their own words.
3. Compare at least two possible improvements using consistent criteria: likely environmental benefits, effects on people and other organisms, resource use, feasibility and tradeoffs. Identify uncertainty and evidence that could change the recommendation.
4. Develop an evidence-based recommendation. Add an annotated photo, map or diagram showing the proposed change and one useful data display where the available evidence supports it. Explain how success could be measured after implementation.
5. Share a draft, answer reviewer questions and revise the recommendation. Record one substantive improvement to the reasoning or proposed change, then present the final report.

**Final product:** One concise illustrated report presented as a short slide deck or equivalent accessible presentation document. It contains the problem, scientific explanation, evidence, comparison of options, recommended change, benefits and tradeoffs, a way to check success, and source credits. Use that same document for the oral presentation rather than assigning an additional long paper. A planning target is about six to eight slides and a four- to six-minute presentation, adjusted during lesson pacing review.

**Individual evidence:** Each student contributes identifiable research and reasoning, explains part of the recommendation, answers a scientific question and describes how feedback improved the proposal. A shared presentation does not replace individual evidence.

**Learning to preserve:** Sustainability and resource choices, energy technology tradeoffs, human effects on ecosystems, and biodiversity design with evaluation and improvement. Retain SCI.16–19 as the recovered home assignments and the listed recurrence assignments; verify exact NGSS routes and Tennessee coverage during curriculum planning. All students receive the required resource, energy and biodiversity instruction even when their chosen problem emphasizes only one area. Use short source comparisons and evidence checks within the lessons for components the final report does not naturally demonstrate. Where a standard requires investigation or testing beyond research and proposal revision, specify that learning task explicitly rather than claiming the presentation alone satisfies it.

**Evidence and assessment:** Judge scientific accuracy, relevant evidence, fair comparison, a justified recommendation and meaningful revision. Distinguish observed findings from predicted benefits; do not invent results for an improvement that has not been implemented. Evaluate clarity of communication separately from decorative design. Actual construction, purchasing and implementation are not required final products.

**Scope:** One park problem, two possible improvements, one recommendation and one report used for presentation. Keep research sources and supporting responses bounded. Develop the source packet, models, feedback prompts and task-specific assessment criteria during the lesson build. This is a research and presentation project, with no simulation, game rounds, event cards or game scores.

#### H3 — Ancient Science: Inventions, Sky and Navigation

**Student challenge:** How did people in ancient China and Greece use observations and inventions to understand their world, and how can we compare their methods using evidence?

**Project type:** Historical investigation with a simple scientific demonstration. Students study both societies, then investigate one shared problem: telling time, observing sky patterns, or finding direction. The default comparison is timekeeping through shadows and water flow. A sky-observation or navigation comparison may replace it when reliable, appropriately dated sources and an accessible investigation are available.

**Historical anchors:** Han China provides documented cases of water clocks, sundials and astronomical instruments. Greek astronomy provides a contrasting case of using observations and mathematical models; the Antikythera mechanism is a source-analysis example of astronomical prediction, not a required reconstruction or an assumed navigation device. Use the [Metropolitan Museum's Han overview](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/han-dynasty-206-b-c-220-a-d) and the [Science Museum's account of ancient Greek computing](https://blog.sciencemuseum.org.uk/ancient-greek-computing/) as initial teacher references. Select and verify exact instrument examples, dates and source excerpts during curriculum planning. Do not place later inventions or uses in the ancient period without evidence.

**What students do:**

1. Establish a map and timeline for China and Greece. Learn the required dynasties, philosophies, city-states, governance and conflicts, connecting these to who supported knowledge, which problems mattered and whose contributions are recorded.
2. Examine a bounded, teacher-selected source set with at least one documented case from each society. Identify the practical problem, observations, instrument or model, users and limits of the historical evidence. Distinguish an artifact from a later reconstruction or modern explanation.
3. Conduct one manageable modern investigation of the selected principle. For the default timekeeping comparison, record repeated shadow-position or water-flow observations under specified conditions. Use safe materials, units, a simple data table, consistent conditions and an accessible equivalent. A modern classroom model explores a principle; its performance does not establish an ancient instrument's exact accuracy.
4. Compare the methods using the same questions: What is measured? How does the method work? What conditions affect it? What could it help people do? What are its limits? Support historical claims with sources and scientific claims with observations or clearly attributed data. Avoid ranking an entire civilization as more advanced from one device.
5. Explain how knowledge could travel through documented exchange networks, including the Silk Road and Hellenistic connections in their own periods. Distinguish evidence of exchange from independent similarity; a similar invention does not prove direct transmission between China and Greece.
6. Share the demonstration and comparison, respond to questions and revise one claim that needs stronger evidence or a clearer limitation.

**Final product:** One compact paired investigation display, physical or digital, with a China panel, a Greece panel, a dated map/timeline, a comparison table, the investigation data and source credits. Students explain one simple demonstration during a short walkthrough. Reuse the same display for presentation; a separate long report is not required.

**Individual evidence:** Each student explains one historically supported achievement from each society, interprets an observation or data pattern, identifies a limitation and explains one institutional or geographic influence on the development or exchange of ideas. Assess historical reasoning, scientific comparison and communication separately.

**Learning to preserve:** Retain SS.16–19 and the listed recurrence assignments. Scientific achievements provide the organizing project context; explicit instruction and individual evidence still address China and Greece's required geography, governance, philosophies, conflict and diffusion. NCSS and C3 guide the social studies curriculum. Verify any science standards contribution against the actual investigation before claiming it; shared subject matter alone does not establish coverage.

**Scope and material handoff:** Both societies, one shared problem, one practical investigation and one display. Build bounded source excerpts, an investigation protocol, verified example data, model/developing comparisons and assessment guidance during the curriculum build. Confirm dates, attribution, safe procedures, access and pacing before teaching. Navigation is an optional evidence-supported focus rather than a requirement to add a second investigation.
