# Curriculum planning step guide

## Purpose and structure

Maintain this authoritative process guide under the [Curriculum Brain Instructions](curriculum-brain.md). The README is a file index; it does not govern curriculum design.

Develop a class and subject's scope and sequence and lessons from the grade's completed 16-project set supplied by the user. Project creation and selection happen before this guide begins. Step 1 receives the completed project handoff; it does not repeat the upstream standards-to-project selection process.

Work on one subject at a time using its four supplied projects. Each lesson belongs to one of those projects; instruction, practice, assessment and materials stay within its sequence. Coordinate dependencies with other subjects without requiring all 16 projects' detailed lessons to be developed first.

**Separate workflows:** `Projects all 16 creation` is the upstream project-creation area, not a curriculum input directory. Once the completed grade project set is handed off, do not load its creation instructions, historical audits, draft inventories or intermediate designs. Do not use recursive searches through that folder to fill curriculum decisions. Reopen it only when the user explicitly requests project-creation work. The only input carried forward is the completed project handoff, stored in the grade folder when supplied.

Older curriculum documents may cite creation-stage inventories or handoff reviews as provenance. Those citations are historical, not instructions to reload or apply the creation process. This boundary governs curriculum work even when an older file calls those records its source of record.

**Handoff content:** The final set should identify grade, project ID/title, subject, sequence, purpose, student role, final experience, assigned learning/standards and essential dependencies or constraints. Preserve decisions actually supplied; do not import all creation-stage paperwork or treat a draft as approved. If the final handoff lacks necessary information, record the specific missing input and use the user's supplied decisions and current curriculum files for unaffected work. Do not silently reconstruct an approved set from the creation archive.

## Standards policy: national design, Tennessee compliance

Build learning targets, progression, teaching and assessments from the selected national standards. Use Tennessee standards to check content compliance after the initial learning map and again before publishing. Do not build two curricula or require a lesson-by-lesson essay about differences. Identify only gaps that change what must be taught, practiced or assessed, then repair those gaps within the projects.

| Subject | National design basis |
| --- | --- |
| ELA | [Common Core ELA and literacy](https://www.thecorestandards.org/ELA-Literacy/), including the applicable grade-level reading, writing, speaking/listening and language expectations |
| Math | [Common Core mathematics](https://corestandards.org/mathematics-standards/), including mathematical practices |
| Science | [NGSS](https://www.nextgenscience.org/search-standards), with a documented allocation of middle-school expectations across Grades 6–8 |
| Social studies | [NCSS National Curriculum Standards for Social Studies](https://www.socialstudies.org/standards/national-curriculum-standards-social-studies) with [C3 inquiry guidance](https://www.socialstudies.org/standards/c3). Specify course content and progression alongside the themes and inquiry skills. |

Record the selected editions and grade/course allocation. Preserve supplied Forge IDs and project assignments as traceability records; an older Tennessee-centered crosswalk does not determine the new curriculum's design basis. Reconcile the complete national scope with supplied assignments and flag any ownership or calendar decision needed. Do not silently drop existing required learning during the transition.

Maintain one compact Tennessee compliance table in the course appendix: **TN requirement/component | lesson and individual evidence destination | status | action needed**. Use **Covered in plan**, **Gap**, or **Unverified**. Covered means the requirement has a defensible route in the curriculum, not that students have mastered it. Review every applicable requirement; in the summary report only gaps, unresolved checks and necessary corrections. No equivalence percentages, closest-match ranking or routine prose about harmless wording differences. Add Tennessee-specific requirements where necessary without changing the national design basis.

Maintain accreditation evidence separately against the school's selected accreditor and supplied criteria. Record its identity and requirements when available; do not equate national content alignment, Tennessee content compliance, NSQ quality guidance and accreditation status. This guide governs curriculum planning; the Tennessee table is a content check, not a determination of all school operating requirements.

| Requirement | Plan |
| --- | --- |
| Current Grade 6 subjects | ELA, math, science, social studies |
| Projects per class | Four supplied projects |
| Each project | Four weeks; eight 90-minute class blocks |
| Each week | One individual block and one group block |
| Each class | 16 weeks; 32 blocks; 48 classroom hours |

Use the actual instructional calendar. These totals describe the four-project sequence, not necessarily an entire school year. Identify remaining weeks and their purpose. List outside work separately and flag any classroom time shortage.

## Run the work efficiently

Use the existing nine-column teacher workbook as the default. Establish the audience, requested stopping point and source baseline before expanding content. Do not redesign the format for each subject; adapt the resources, instruction and evidence within it.

1. **Set the starting brief once (Step 1).** Record supplied projects, standards editions, schedule, learner assumptions, resource constraints and requested deliverables. Reuse verified source records; recheck when the intended year, edition or source changes.
2. **Combine learning mapping, project design and course review (Steps 2–4).** Maintain one component-level learning map and one 32-lesson outline. These steps resolve different decisions, not three versions of the same prose. If only a subset is requested, complete that scope.
3. **Check the busiest individual/group pair before expanding.** Select it from new learning, reading, product and observation demands, not simply because it comes first. Populate the established workbook fields for this pair and walk through instruction, tasks, evidence, support and handoff. At Step 4, test the outline; when Step 5 is authorized, develop the pair fully before the other lessons. Resolve routine choices and continue without an extra approval checkpoint.
4. **Develop and review each pair (Step 5).** Fix alignment, resources, directions, assessment and pacing while drafting. Then check all eight lessons and the course progression. Do not defer these checks until the user requests an audit.
5. **Publish synchronized views once the content is stable.** Produce the course map and concise workbook from the maintained lesson records. Reopen only decisions affected by a correction; regenerate and check affected views.
6. **Produce materials and validate use (Step 6).** Keep curriculum judgment, file consistency and material readiness distinct. Carry remaining needs forward with exact actions.

When the user requests a complete planning build, run Steps 1–5 together. A request for scope and sequence alone ends at Step 4 unless detailed lessons are also requested. A sample pair is an internal quality check, not permission to stop before completing the authorized scope. Measure efficiency by substantive revisions avoided and useful outputs completed; do not promise a build duration based on one earlier run.

## Plain-English directions and project grading

Write the scope and sequence and lesson-planning workbook for the teacher who will build the lessons. Use plain, understandable language to explain what to prepare, teach, model, practice and assess. Student-friendly wording belongs in the project/product descriptions and actual student task directions; it does not turn the entire planning workbook into a student handout. Explain necessary subject terms and translate standards into concrete learning. Keep official identifiers and detailed crosswalks in their designated reference locations.

Develop every lesson in the requested scope to the same level of detail. These rules apply to all four projects, not just the first example. A lesson needs enough information for a teacher to build the activity and for a student to understand the work. Name the text or problem, explain the sequence of actions, identify the work to save, and connect it to the project's final product. Avoid empty headings, unexplained resource codes, generic “complete the activity” directions and repeated descriptions that could fit any lesson.

In **Student work, assessment, feedback and support**, replace generic success criteria with a different, task-specific explanation for each lesson. Name the work to collect, observable qualities, feedback/revision and learning supports. Explain whether the evidence is practice, a draft, a final product or a new application. Do not substitute “apply the rubric,” “meet the standards,” or a list of codes for the explanation. Student handouts may use the heading **How your project work is graded**; the teacher workbook uses the combined planning column.

For example: **Collect each student's interpretation and revision. Check that the interpretation fits the story, uses a relevant passage and explains the connection. Check that the revision improves the reasoning. Review individual evidence separately from the team's case board.** Adapt the criteria to the actual task; do not paste this example into every lesson.

Use individual work to judge each student's learning. Give separate feedback on the team's product or presentation. Explain how students use feedback and what they can revise or try again. Record missing evidence honestly. Do not invent point totals, grade weights, letter-grade cutoffs or passing scores without a supplied school policy. Plain-language grading explains how quality is judged; it does not create a new school grading policy. Simplifying the wording must not remove required instruction, individual evidence, accessible participation or necessary new-task checks.

## Workbook model and synchronization

Use five sheets: **Course overview**, followed by **Project 1**, **Project 2**, **Project 3** and **Project 4**. The course sheet contains the overview fields and the five-column project overview specified above. Each project sheet has eight lesson rows across four project weeks, in individual-then-group order. These are eight lessons, not eight weeks per project.

Use these lesson columns in this order:

| Column | Heading and content |
| --- | --- |
| A | **Lesson** — Combine lesson ID, project week and lesson type in one cell, for example `E1-W1-I` followed by `Week 1 · Individual`. Do not restore three separate columns. |
| B | **Lesson focus and project connection** — Combine the lesson idea, plain-language learning purpose and contribution to the project. |
| C | **Texts, resources and reading plan** — Recognizable titles, working links, assigned complete texts or bounded sections, reading workload and access support. Mark provisional selections. |
| D | **Instruction and modeling** — What the teacher explicitly teaches, demonstrates and guides before independent work. Specify the example to prepare. |
| E | **Lesson activity design** — The ordered activity sequence the teacher will build, including prompts, student actions and use of prior work. |
| F | **Student work, assessment, feedback and support** — Work to collect, task-specific quality criteria, practice/final status, feedback and revision, access supports and extension. Keep the parts labeled within one cell. |
| G | **Preparation and next-lesson handoff** — Specific materials still to create or verify and the work/decisions carried into the next lesson. Do not present planned materials as finished. |
| H | **Skills taught and checked** — Four short parts: **Teach / revisit**, **Practice**, **Check**, and **Builds on**. Translate the lesson's priority standards into concrete teaching and observable individual learning. |
| I | **Standards — national** — Selected national content references and lesson roles, kept last. Name the framework. Tennessee compliance belongs in the course appendix, not a second main lesson column. |

Within the national standards cell, record two separate judgments for each reference. **Lesson role: Primary or Supporting.** Primary identifies central teaching or deliberate review with identifiable individual evidence; Supporting identifies a skill used or reinforced during the lesson. **Plan verification: Confirmed in the plan or Conditional.** Confirmed means the teaching/practice/evidence route is specified and defensible at the reviewed stage. Conditional means a named feature or connection still needs verification. For example: **Primary · Conditional — the selected passage still needs to contain the narrator feature required by the prompt.** Conditional is a verification status, not a third lesson role or a synonym for weak importance. These labels fit in the existing cell; do not add columns.

Track material readiness and observed student learning separately from plan verification. A sound activity specification can be confirmed in the plan while its worksheet is still pending Step 6. A cumulative expectation, such as reading range, can have a confirmed contribution in this lesson and still require evidence across the term. Name that cumulative route; do not automatically classify every cumulative standard as conditional. Preserve required assignments, and never claim a match from the topic or resource title alone. If no defensible role exists yet, record **Role unresolved · Conditional**, name the repair or destination decision, and do not count verified coverage.

Below the lessons, keep a learning-evidence review table: **Lesson | Primary national standards and evidence | Supporting national standards | Plan verification / limits**. Evaluate the taught/checked component rather than claiming whole-standard mastery. Identify planned, materials-produced and observed evidence accurately. Name the missing action for an unsupported connection. This table checks lesson quality; the separate Tennessee table checks content compliance.

Use visible text labels, not color alone. The blank template retains the table structure and legend, with curriculum-entry cells empty. Standards differences warrant attention only when they change a required learning or evidence destination.

Do not include **Bring and get ready**, **Course week**, an additional **Common Core reference**, **Agenda & timing** or **Project work (minutes)** as main lesson columns. Common Core belongs in the national standards field for ELA/math. Keep project week/type in the Lesson cell and timing in the teacher reference. Resource and assessment references may sit below the lessons. Do not claim NGSS coverage merely because an ELA reading has a science topic.

**Writing Skills taught and checked:** Explain the learning, not just the assignment or standard code. For example: **Teach / revisit:** Separate stated facts from inferences. **Practice:** Model one evidence note, then use another passage. **Check:** The student selects a relevant quotation and explains how it supports an inference. **Builds on:** Locating directly stated information. Use the actual lesson content and prerequisites for all 32 rows; do not repeat a generic entry. This column gives a concise learning progression. Column F names the actual product, assessment criteria and feedback; column E supplies the full activity sequence. Retain all assigned standards and detailed component routes in the references even when the main cell highlights only the lesson's priorities. Keep the blank template's H cells empty.

**Avoid repetition between fields:** B answers why this lesson belongs here. C identifies what resources and selections it uses. D explains the teacher's demonstration and guided instruction. E gives the ordered activity procedure. F identifies work, quality judgments and the response to difficulty. G identifies unfinished preparation and the next handoff. H summarizes the learning progression in four short statements; it does not repeat the procedure or rubric. I identifies official references and match judgments. State detailed procedures, models and rubrics once in the project teaching document and link or summarize them in the workbook. Do not paste the same paragraph into several cells or increase detail merely to fill a field.

Keep text wrapped and readable, with enough row height for the actual content. Freeze the lesson header and the single Lesson column. Preserve source links when moving columns. Check the final saved workbook, not just a screenshot or an export-success message.

Keep lesson cells concise enough to scan at normal zoom. Summarize the actual instruction, activity and assessment, then link to the full procedure in the project document. Do not copy multi-paragraph lesson scripts into cells, shrink fonts to fit, or substitute vague summaries for essential planning information. Inspect a populated pair before expansion and the longest final rows afterward. Correct density by removing repetition and relocating detail, not by dropping required learning.

When an approved structural change affects the reusable model, update both the completed class workbook and the blank template. Leave the blank template's curriculum-entry cells empty; retain only headings and structural lesson/week/type labels. Keep the class document, project lesson documents and completed workbook consistent when content changes. Do not fill the blank template with the class's examples.

## Resource names, links and preparation

Use a resource's full, recognizable title and author or organization in each lesson. A code such as `S1` is an optional cross-reference, not a usable description by itself. Write **S1 — Aaron Steil, Iowa State — How to Create a Pollinator Lawn**, rather than only “S1.” Supply a direct link to the full source and state the assigned section separately. A link to a complete book or article does not assign the entire publication.

In project documents, place named source links with the relevant lesson directions. In the workbook, show titles in the lesson row. If a cell cannot hold multiple distinct clickable links, link it to that lesson's clearly labeled resource list on the same sheet; give each source its own clickable link there. Include its code, title, assigned selection and preparation status. A general note saying “see the register below” without usable names and navigation is insufficient.

For teacher-created materials, link the actual file once it is produced. Until then, label the item **Pending Step 6** and describe what must be prepared. Do not create a fake link, present a source article as the finished activity sheet, or label an unbuilt assessment case as ready. Keep fresh-reading sections held back where the plan requires a new application. Record source/edition, author, URL, excerpt boundaries, permissions/access checks and reading estimates in the resource reference.

## Step 1 — Receive the projects for the class and subject

**Needs:** The completed grade project handoff, selected subject, official national standards, Tennessee compliance references, calendar, class schedule and known student needs.

**Do:** Record the final handoff's project IDs, titles, sequence, student roles, goals, final experiences and assigned learning, including primary and recurring standards when supplied. Carry forward essential teaching/evidence requirements explicitly included in that final handoff. Identify missing inputs without inventing replacement projects or consulting creation-stage drafts.

For Grade 6, use the [recovered project handoff](<6th Grade/Grade_6_Project_Handoff.md>) identified by the user and select the subject's four projects. It preserves recovered project decisions and their original planning status; verify national alignment during curriculum development. For subsequent work on an already developed subject, use its current curriculum records alongside the handoff. Official standards supply the learning requirements. Historical Forge exports and creation-stage inventories are not required inputs and must not be loaded automatically. Preserve identifiers already carried into the current records without reopening their creation history.

Record available time, class size when known, resources, student starting levels, access needs, and outside-work constraints. State the teacher audience and requested stopping point. Distinguish confirmed information from assumptions and unresolved decisions. Use one unresolved-needs list with **affected lesson/component | missing information or action | owner if known | consequence for readiness**; link to it rather than repeating warnings throughout the outputs.

**Standards source and version record:** Before mapping, record each framework's issuing authority, official title, grade/subject, source URL and saved file, edition or revision/adoption date, implementation school year where applicable, and date checked. Identify the national design basis and the Tennessee compliance edition separately. The selected Tennessee school-year baseline is currently 2027–28; verify the applicable edition when that changes. Do not silently mix editions. Record unknown dates or transitions and draft unaffected portions provisionally. Keep the record in the starting brief/reference section, not new lesson columns.

Use a documented provisional assumption where work can proceed. Missing inputs prevent a readiness claim, but do not prevent drafting independent portions of the sequence. Ask for a decision only when it changes project identity, required scope, or an affected choice that cannot reasonably be drafted.

**Produces:** One starting brief for the selected class and subject, containing its supplied projects, standards assignments, source/version record, schedule, and unresolved needs.

**Check:** The projects are received inputs. This step does not create, select, or redesign them, and does not claim their lessons or materials are complete.

## Step 2 — Decide what students must learn and show

**Needs:** The starting brief, official source standards and the completed project handoff's learning assignments.

**Do:** Unpack the selected national standards into assessable components and reconcile them with each project's supplied assignments. For each component, identify:

- What students must know, understand, and do, expressed as a clear learning target.
- Prerequisites, likely difficulties, and support needs.
- Instruction, practice, and revisiting needed within this and later projects.
- Individual evidence that demonstrates the required depth of learning.

Keep source wording separate from planning interpretations. Preserve primary and recurring assignments. Flag gaps or assignment conflicts for resolution rather than silently changing the supplied projects.

Required content that does not naturally fit the project's theme still receives explicit teaching, practice, and individual evidence inside that project's lessons and time budget. It need not be forced into the final product. Do not create a separate lesson sequence outside the projects.

**Produces:** A learning map for each project, with stable standard and target references and identified prerequisites between projects.

**Check:** Every assigned component has planned teaching and individual evidence. Team roles and products do not replace each student's required learning.

Run the first Tennessee gap check against this national learning map before expanding lessons. Add any required missing component and its proposed destination to the same map. Resolve assignment conflicts explicitly; do not create a separate Tennessee lesson sequence.

**Distinct job:** Resolve learning requirements before designing activities. Use one compact map with **standard/component ID | required learning | prerequisite | teaching/practice/check lesson IDs | individual evidence | later recurrence | unresolved need**. Begin with project destinations and refine them to lesson IDs during Steps 3–4 in the same map. Identify what is newly taught and what returns for practice. Do not write lesson scripts, repeat the project description, or manufacture a separate assignment for each standard. One meaningful piece of work can demonstrate several components if each remains visible. Identify missing skills, prerequisites or evidence requirements, or confirm that none was found. Keep official wording in the teacher appendix.

## Step 3 — Design a workable eight-lesson project

**Needs:** The project learning maps, supplied goals and final experiences, and available time.

**Do:** Give each project an essential question and two or three lasting understandings that express its supplied purpose. Before setting weekly milestones, specify success criteria for the supplied final experience and for each student's required learning. Work backward to four weekly milestones, naming two or three principal learning priorities each week. Identify supporting practice separately. Include individual contribution, group work, feedback, revision, and evidence. Retain the supplied project's identity and purpose; flag any change that requires a user decision.

Apply the subject-specific directions below during mapping, sequencing, detailed planning and material production.

Identify needed resources and cross-subject prerequisites. Do not assume another class has already taught a skill without a confirmed handoff.

**Produces:** Success criteria and four weekly milestones containing eight lesson outlines for each supplied project: one individual and one group lesson per week. Each outline names the learning, assigned resource/problem, student action, saved work and feedback/revision. Step 4 reviews and publishes this same sequence; it does not create a competing version.

**Check:** Instruction prepares students for the work ahead. Teaching, reading, practice, creation, feedback, revision, and assessment can fit within eight blocks. Spread first instruction across lessons; do not stack numerous new skills into a single week. Combine evidence in meaningful work when each component remains visible. Trial reading, writing, transitions, and observation demands; arithmetic alone does not establish classroom feasibility. Resolve pacing gaps without dropping standards or silently adding time.

**Early workload check:** Compare all four projects before expanding lesson prose. Count unfamiliar concepts and procedures, resource difficulty, required individual evidence and teacher observation demands; standard counts alone do not measure workload. Equal eight-block allocations can contain very unequal learning loads. Select the busiest pair and account for first reading, modeling, guided turns, project work, independent checks, transitions, feedback and reteaching. For conferences or presentations, estimate students/groups multiplied by observation time and show how the teacher can collect each required observation. Do not double-book the teacher or count the same support window for incompatible tasks. Mark unknown class-size and readiness assumptions.

Remove duplicate products and decorative work, reuse evidence, and redistribute tasks within the supplied sequence first. Preserve at least 30 minutes of actual project work per block. If required learning still will not fit, identify the affected components and specific calendar or assignment decision needed. Do not silently move standards between home projects, extend blocks, assume mastery on entry or transfer essential work to homework. A planning walkthrough is provisional until actual student timing and evidence are available.

**Distinct job:** Make the practical design decisions. Produce an eight-row lesson outline naming **main learning | assigned reading/problem | student action | piece saved | feedback/revision**. Define one main product and bounded supporting pieces, with a draft and revision before the final version. Set local size expectations, reading sections and planned support; these are planning choices, not official grading cutoffs. Explain what students will do in plain English. Reuse the Step 2 map instead of rewriting standards. Remove duplicate essays, decorative production and unnecessary copying before reducing required learning. This step must change the actual task sequence when work does not fit.

## Step 4 — Review progression and publish the course map

**Needs:** The supplied project order, weekly milestones, learning maps, and calendar.

**Do:** Assemble the class document in the format above. Show all four projects and all 16 weeks. Keep each weekly row short enough to scan: its priorities, individual/group work, and evidence/checkpoint. Use project sections for essential questions, lasting understandings, focus standards, and resources. Use one assessment overview to distinguish major individual work from group products and identify scoring dimensions. Show reading, writing, language/vocabulary, research, and speaking/listening progression for ELA. Keep prerequisites, access assumptions, and outside work visible without repeating them in every row.

Give each lesson a stable ID, such as `E1-W1-I` and `E1-W1-G` for Project E1, Week 1, individual and group lessons. In the project teaching documents, link every learning target to the lesson IDs where it is taught, practiced, and assessed, including recurring learning and later applications. Keep these IDs consistent across the class map, lessons, and materials. Priority standards in the main view do not replace this complete map.

Check the complete supplied subject standards against the sequence, including standards missing from the original project assignments. Flag omissions for resolution without silently changing project assignments. Check learning progression and recurring practice across the four projects. Coordinate shared resources and deadlines with other subjects where information is available; mark unresolved dependencies.

**Required learning-evidence review in Step 4:** For every lesson, populate **Skills taught and checked** and record each national reference's lesson role (**Primary/Supporting**) separately from plan verification (**Confirmed in the plan/Conditional**). Use Role unresolved when needed. Base roles on teaching, practice and individual evidence, not the topic. Record the evidence reason and prerequisite. Distinguish component coverage from whole-standard coverage; name the exact missing feature for a conditional route.

Complete the learning-evidence review table below each project's lessons and the course-level Tennessee compliance table. Do not copy all project standards into every lesson or label every reference primary. Preserve supporting/cumulative learning with real destinations. Correct unsupported routes or record exact unresolved actions before publishing. Report Tennessee gaps and their repairs without a detailed standards-comparison narrative.

**Produces:** One scope and sequence for the selected class and subject, organized by project, week, and individual/group lesson, plus completed plain-language skill entries and the lesson-level standards match review in the existing workbook/project references.

**Check:** Every required component has a teaching and evidence destination inside a project. The course map is readable without opening its appendix, and the detailed evidence remains traceable. Check prerequisite order and workload. Record unresolved choices once; develop lessons on stated provisional resources where possible, and resolve those choices before calling affected plans final or classroom-ready.

**Step 4 completion requirement:** Every lesson has justified national standards roles and evidence routes. Every applicable Tennessee requirement has a coverage status and destination or named corrective action. A code list alone is incomplete. Open gaps prevent a complete content-compliance claim but do not prevent publishing an honestly labeled draft.

**Distinct job:** Compare all four projects. Check increasing independence, repeated versus missing skills, reading variety and sustained reading, assessment load, handoffs and calendar scope. Look specifically for four projects that repeat the same task at the same difficulty. Fix the affected Step 3 outlines, then publish the concise course map and workbook. Do not re-unpack standards or expand every lesson here. Report the concrete course-level corrections in chat; if none are needed, say so and finish the step without another rewritten planning document.

**Working efficiently across Steps 2–4:** These are three decisions, not three versions of the same prose. When the user authorizes all three, complete them in one workflow and update the same files. A learning requirement belongs to Step 2; a lesson/order/product decision belongs to Step 3; a cross-project/calendar/display decision belongs to Step 4. Reopen only the affected decisions when later evidence changes the plan. Do not count more headings, longer standards lists or repeated audit totals as improvement.

## Step 5 — Develop the lessons inside each project

**Needs:** The class scope and sequence, project learning maps, weekly milestones, and relevant resources.

**Do:** Develop four weekly lesson pairs inside each project: one individual and one group lesson per week, eight lessons per project. Complete the scope the user requests. If the request covers all projects, develop all 32 lessons with full directions and task-specific grading; do not stop after a sample project. If the request names one project, complete its eight lessons. For an unspecified request to start the step, begin with a concrete first project and report what is drafted and what remains. Use the same project teaching documents; do not present empty headings as completed lessons.

For each lesson provide its stable ID, project/week, two or three principal outcomes, supporting targets, prerequisites/starting work, exact or proposed materials, teacher actions, learner tasks, feedback, individual evidence, and next-step handoff. Distinguish newly taught targets from recurring/supporting practice in the teacher reference. Maintain the sequential 90-minute agenda there; do not put it back in the workbook's main lesson view.

Build the teacher plan around the nine workbook fields above. Give an ordered activity sequence detailed enough for a teacher to create the lesson, with specific models, prompts, resources, work to collect and assessment guidance. Keep any student-readable task directions as a supporting section or handout, with simple product descriptions and explained terms. Existing student directions remain useful source material; changing the workbook layout alone does not count as rewriting them into teacher plans. Reuse prior work purposefully instead of assigning duplicate products.

Place the original technical mapping, timing and evidence conditions in a labeled teacher-reference section, optionally expandable, so students do not have to read standards language to understand the lesson. Preserve required learning and source records when rewriting the directions. Full lesson directions are Step 5 work; finished activity sheets, exact new-case texts, answer guidance and calibrated examples remain Step 6 work until actually created and checked.

Use three connected purposes:

1. **Introduce the project phase:** Establish the current situation and milestone.
2. **Teach and practice:** Develop the required learning through explicit instruction and learning modules.
3. **Build and revise:** Apply learning to students' project work.

Allocate assessment, transitions, feedback, and support within each 90-minute block. Protect at least 30 minutes of actual project work in both individual and group lessons. Include required instruction that is outside the theme within these same lessons. Identify outside work separately; flag pacing decisions when the learning does not fit.

Use a budget fitted to the lesson; do not repeat a generic minute pattern without checking its tasks. Count each minute once. Collect evidence during authentic work rather than adding a separate test for every target. Preserve typing and digital collaboration/publication where required, but distinguish the source criterion from a local time allowance. Record actual conditions and access supports.

Specify each learning module's target, learner task, teaching approach, feedback, support, and evidence. Distinguish supported practice from later independent evidence. Include reteaching and extension. Every student must have access to required learning regardless of team role.

**Response to difficulty in every lesson:** Name a likely misconception or obstacle, the student response that would reveal it, the specific teacher action, and a brief recheck using a different example. For instance, if a student copies a quotation without connecting it to a claim, model the connection on another passage, have the student explain the original clue orally, then check a new clue. Use the existing F column for a concise summary and the project document for procedures. In G, identify the next lesson or within-project support window for unfinished learning and what evidence follows the student. Budget this support inside available time. Do not write only “differentiate” or “reteach as needed,” and do not replace the student's assessed reasoning with the teacher's answer.

Specify assessment prompts, required response features, evidence conditions, and observable scoring criteria tied to the success criteria, ready for material production in Step 6. Show how one artifact supplies evidence for multiple targets. Separate individual scores from group product quality, and separate access/support records from achievement judgments. Use descriptive levels unless a school grading policy has been supplied; do not invent a passing score. State reteaching and fresh recheck routes without treating an unobserved component as mastered.

**Required standards-match verification in Step 5:** Recheck the Step 4 classifications against the detailed lesson, not against the earlier outline alone. For each primary match, locate the explicit teaching or deliberate review, the student practice and the individual response the teacher will assess. Confirm the prompt and assessment criteria actually require the named skill at the standard's depth. For supporting matches, identify the actual practice or review. For conditional matches, specify exactly what remains unverified and what evidence would resolve it. Unbuilt materials remain planned evidence, even when their specifications are complete.

If the detailed activity does not support the claimed match, repair the activity and evidence specification within the time budget, or revise the classification and required learning destination. Do not remove required learning, infer it from participation or promote a match to fill a table. Update **Skills taught and checked**, national reference labels and affected course/project entries together. Recheck only the Tennessee compliance routes affected by the change.

**Produces:** Lesson plans, module specifications, and assessment specifications organized inside each supplied project: eight lessons per project and 32 for the class, with standards-match classifications verified against the detailed plans and remaining conditional matches explicitly documented.

**Check:** Every lesson belongs to a project and matches its scope-and-sequence entry. Another teacher can build the lesson without guessing its resources, demonstration, activity sequence, work to collect, quality criteria or response to difficulty. Student-readability checks apply to project/product descriptions and student task directions. Individual learning is assessable, each block fits its teacher-reference clock, and required content is not lost because it does not fit the theme. Check workbook and project documents together; verify resource navigation, standards judgments and consistent handoffs.

### Step 5 build-and-audit cycle

Audit while building each individual/group pair, then audit all eight lessons together. Repair failed checks in actual directions, resources, products and clocks before moving on. A statement of alignment does not demonstrate feasibility. Do not add an extra audit sheet to the teacher-planning workbook; keep one short review table in the teacher reference with **lesson/project | issue | concrete correction | evidence checked | unresolved need**. Apply the common checks to all subjects and the subject-specific checks below to the selected course.

| Audit question | Required evidence and action |
| --- | --- |
| Does each standards-match label hold up? | Trace each primary national target to teaching/review, practice and an individual assessment prompt/criterion. Locate supporting practice and explain conditional limits. Repair weak routes and synchronize outputs. Recheck affected Tennessee coverage without routine counterpart comparisons. Distinguish specified, materials-produced and observed evidence. |
| Is the reading appropriate for this grade and these students? | Inspect the actual assigned section, not its title or topic. Record language, structure, knowledge demands and reader/task needs. Use a suitable quantitative measure when helpful, without inventing Lexiles or treating a poem's short length as proof of ease. Distinguish guided, partner and independent reading. Name vocabulary/context support and how support fades. Keep demanding thinking while making the text accessible. |
| Is the reading mix balanced? | Check genre, period, voice and repeated authors across the course. Include contemporary perspectives and a sustained-reading route. Replace an equivalent selection instead of automatically adding readings. A simple fable can support comparison but cannot alone establish grade-band reading evidence. A novel is not automatically required. |
| Can students complete the project in the available time? | Name one main product and define each supporting response's size and purpose. Count first reading, rereading, planning, drafting, feedback, revision, transitions and presentation/observation. Track all new texts and unfamiliar operations in the busiest block. Reserve time for comprehension difficulties. Reuse notes and collect evidence during work. Trim duplicate writing and polish; redistribute tasks within the eight lessons. Do not hide shortages in homework. |
| Can another teacher build this lesson without guessing? | Locate the selected resources, model/example specification, key prompts, activity sequence, expected work and quality criteria. Identify exactly what remains to make. Confirm that D, E, F and H serve different purposes rather than repeating prose. |
| Are the student-facing portions understandable? | Check only the project/product description and actual student task directions for grade-appropriate wording, first action, assigned selection, finished piece and quality expectations. Explain unfamiliar subject terms. Do not turn the teacher plan into a student handout. |
| Is the response to difficulty actionable? | Identify the likely obstacle, observable sign, teacher response, different-example recheck and destination for unfinished learning. Check that support has time and preserves the intended thinking. |
| Is grading clear and proportionate? | Mark practice, draft, final product and new application. Give task-specific observable qualities and examples of what needs improvement. Keep individual evidence separate from team polish. Do not grade exact word counts or typing speed unless an authorized policy explicitly requires it. Do not invent weights or cutoffs. |
| Are models and materials buildable? | Specify a worked example on different content, a developing response with feedback, and a complete example matching the expected product size. List exact materials still to produce. A planned example is not an existing resource. Produce and calibrate these in Step 6. |
| Do the files agree? | Compare every lesson's reading, action, output, grading, timing and handoff in the project document and workbook. Update resource registers, links, weekly map and target destinations after moves. Keep the blank template blank; change it only if the shared structure changes. |

### Subject-specific design and review directions

Apply these from the initial learning map onward, not just at the final audit. Record findings in the existing review table and resources in the project register; add no subject-specific workbook columns. Reading access and individual evidence matter in all four subjects.

#### ELA — Common Core ELA and literacy

- **Select texts before expanding lessons.** Record title, author, genre, edition/link, complete-text or exact excerpt boundaries, instructional purpose, approximate word count, reading/re-reading time and access status. Check language, structure, background knowledge and reader/task needs; use a quantitative measure when helpful without inventing a Lexile. Draft with marked provisional choices, but verify the actual selections before final material production.
- **Sequence all strands.** Map evidence-based reading, literature and informational texts, narrative/explanatory/argument writing, language/vocabulary, research, speaking and listening across the projects. Distinguish explicit teaching from incidental use. Build increasing independence and revisit learning with new texts. Do not assume a science topic supplies scientific instruction or that group discussion supplies individual listening evidence.
- **Balance the reading plan.** Review genre, period, author and voice across the course, including contemporary perspectives. Plan sustained reading and return to connected texts; a novel is not automatically required. Replace an equivalent selection when improving variety rather than adding unbudgeted reading. Specify guided, partner and independent routes and how support fades.
- **Bound products and instruction.** Use one main product per project, purposeful short supporting responses, and drafts that become revisions. Specify a different-text teacher model, guided practice and an independent response. Teach sentence, convention and vocabulary choices in useful contexts while preserving required explicit practice. Distinguish analysis, summary, explanation and argument in prompts and criteria.
- **Collect disciplinary evidence.** Require a defensible interpretation or claim, relevant passage/source evidence and the student's explanation of the connection. Include individual speaking/listening observations and fresh-text checks where the target calls for transfer. Specify how research sources are evaluated and credited. Record access supports separately from the student's reasoning.
- **Stress-test the busiest pair.** Count first reading, rereading, comprehension support, discussion, drafting, feedback, revision and teacher observation. Time complete tasks rather than relying on word-count estimates. In Step 6, verify excerpt access/reuse, produce accessible texts and held-back assessments, and calibrate model/developing responses against the intended criteria.

#### Math — Common Core mathematics and mathematical practices

- **Map prerequisites and full components.** Connect each target to prior concepts and required representations, procedures and contexts. Distinguish introduction, practice, fluency development and transfer. Include standards components that do not fit the theme; do not infer coverage from a project involving money, measurement or graphs.
- **Sequence understanding and methods.** Move from meaningful situations and concrete/visual representations to symbols and efficient methods as appropriate. Show why procedures work, then provide repeated purposeful practice and varied applications. Require observable mathematical practices, such as constructing an argument or checking a model, rather than attaching practice codes alone.
- **Prepare concrete cases.** Specify numbers, units, diagrams, constraints, a worked example on different values, likely wrong reasoning and a fresh individual problem. Check the arithmetic and whether the project's constraints admit a feasible solution. Keep numerical complexity focused on the intended grade-level mathematics rather than accidental computation burdens.
- **Assess more than answers.** Collect the representation, method, explanation, unit interpretation or reasonableness check the target requires. Gather repeated evidence for fluency. Use private reasoning within team work, and avoid making every component a separate test. Keep short retrieval practice and targeted reteaching inside the clock.
- **Check uneven loads early.** Compare new concepts and evidence demands across projects, not just the count of codes. Test the most crowded algebra/representation pair before expansion. Do not depend on weak entry skills becoming secure in a brief opening task; identify the specific support or calendar need.
- **Produce and verify materials.** In Step 6, independently solve examples and fresh cases, check alternate valid methods and answer guidance, and test diagrams, labels and changed constraints. Tennessee checks add only genuinely missing required content; they do not redirect the course into a standards-comparison exercise.

#### Science — NGSS

- **Allocate the grade band deliberately.** Record which middle-school performance expectations this grade/course owns, what earlier learning it assumes and what later grades complete. Use the complete expectation, its relevant clarification/assessment boundaries and the associated science and engineering practices, disciplinary core ideas and crosscutting concepts. Do not claim all Grades 6–8 expectations in one Grade 6 term.
- **Start from a phenomenon or design problem.** Name what students observe and need to explain or improve. Sequence initial ideas/questions, needed science knowledge, investigation or data analysis, modeling/explanation, critique and revision. Include engineering criteria, constraints and test/revision cycles where relevant to assigned expectations.
- **Make the investigation buildable.** Specify the question, measurements, variables/comparison conditions where appropriate, units, trials, recording method, equipment and safe procedures. Budget setup, observation time, repeated trials and cleanup. Identify an accessible alternative and what it can demonstrate if equipment or live investigation is unavailable. Label simulated or supplied data honestly.
- **Connect evidence to scientific reasoning.** Require each student to interpret data, explain a pattern/mechanism, justify a model or revise an explanation. Address uncertainty, limitations and plausible alternative explanations at an appropriate level. A working device, attractive model or watched demonstration is not sufficient evidence of the assigned performance.
- **Check practical dependencies.** Confirm materials, supervision, environmental conditions, measurement tools and any mathematical prerequisites before detailed production. Do not assume another subject already taught a needed graph or calculation. Check that observation periods fit the weekly schedule and that every student can contribute and produce individual evidence.
- **Produce and verify materials.** In Step 6, trial the procedure, check scientific accuracy and data plausibility, produce accessible diagrams/data sheets and scoring examples, and verify the activity actually elicits the specified dimensions. Use Tennessee only to identify additional required content or grade-placement gaps needing action.

#### Social studies — NCSS with C3 inquiry guidance

- **Specify content as well as inquiry.** Use NCSS themes to organize the course and C3 to develop questions, apply disciplinary concepts/tools, evaluate sources/use evidence, and communicate conclusions/take informed action where appropriate. State the actual periods, places, peoples, events and civic/economic/geographic ideas to learn. Themes and inquiry labels alone do not supply a complete content sequence.
- **Build context before interpretation.** Sequence chronology, geography and essential background before asking students to explain change, causation or perspectives. Connect historical, geographic, civic and economic reasoning to the supplied project. Use maps and timelines as analytical tools with source/date information, not decoration.
- **Select a bounded source set.** Identify creator, date, place, purpose, audience, provenance and assigned excerpt for each source. Distinguish primary/secondary sources, later translations or adaptations, and modern interpretations. Include relevant contrasting perspectives and contemporary scholarship where appropriate; contextualize claims and evaluate evidence rather than treating every claim as equally supported.
- **Teach source reasoning explicitly.** Model sourcing, contextualization and corroboration on a different example. Guide students to compare accounts, notice missing perspectives and explain limits. Require accurate disciplinary vocabulary and evidence-based explanation; separate supported inference, historical interpretation and personal opinion.
- **Make products demonstrate learning.** For an exhibit, annotated map, hearing or exploration, define the individual explanation each student contributes and how its sources support it. Assess historical/social understanding and reasoning separately from visual polish or performance. Bound research, captions and presentations; build feedback and revision into the same product.
- **Stress-test and verify.** Count background instruction, map work, source reading, corroboration, writing and observations. In Step 6, check dates, locations, quotations, image provenance and source access, and produce model/developing explanations. Check Tennessee's selected implementation-year content against the finished plan, especially when supplied assignments came from an older edition; record and repair gaps without substituting Tennessee for the NCSS/C3 design basis.

Use three practical review passes: **teacher build walkthrough** (locate resources and specify instruction, activities, evidence and responses to difficulty), **student-task walkthrough** (check the student-facing portions and expected work), and **workload walkthrough** (simulate the busiest pair's disciplinary tasks and teacher observation demands). The builder may perform these; report them as planning walkthroughs, not classroom trials. Replace timing and learning assumptions with observed student evidence after use.

**Completion language:** Step 5 can be **drafted with unresolved checks** or **reviewed and ready for Step 6 material production**. Use the latter only when planning failures are repaired and remaining needs are genuinely material production or classroom validation. Do not call a plan classroom-ready until text/access checks, actual materials, examples, calendar and practical trial needs are resolved. If all required learning still cannot fit after simplification, describe the specific shortage and the calendar or scope decision needed.

**Step 5 completion requirement:** Do not mark the requested scope reviewed while a primary national target lacks a defensible teaching/practice/assessment route or a Tennessee gap is presented as covered. Report concrete corrections and remaining needs in chat. Step 4 establishes the routes; Step 5 verifies them against detailed lessons; Step 6 checks produced materials and later classroom evidence.

**Final quality test:** Another teacher can identify what students learn, why it comes next, what to prepare, how to teach it, what evidence to collect and what to do when students struggle. A longer document or a populated standards table does not by itself satisfy this test.

## Step 6 — Walk through, create, and verify

**Needs:** The project-based scope and sequence and lesson plans.

**Do:** Walk through one target from its source standard to instruction, practice, and individual assessment inside a project. Walk through an individual/group lesson pair to test timing and handoffs. Fix gaps before expanding production.

Create the specified teaching materials, learning modules, project resources, and assessments within their project lessons. Include the actual assessment prompts and scoring guidance in the completed project materials. Check answer guidance, accessibility, source accuracy, materials, and instructional capabilities. Verify the learner experience before calling the project classroom-ready.

After classroom use, compare timing and learning with student work. Update affected lessons and scope-and-sequence entries together.

**Produces:** Verified teaching materials and assessments within the supplied projects, with unresolved needs clearly identified.

**Check:** Standards coverage, lesson quality, and material readiness are checked separately. Planned or unbuilt activities are not counted as completed materials.
