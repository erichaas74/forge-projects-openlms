# Grade 6 ELA scope and sequence

**Current status — October 2, 2026:** Step 5 planning is drafted for all four supplied projects: 32 detailed lesson drafts with modules and assessment specifications across 16 weeks. Step 6 teaching materials, text/access verification, calendar confirmation and classroom pacing/observation trials remain open. This is a planning draft, not yet classroom-ready. **Baseline:** Tennessee ELA for 2027–28. **Owner/approval:** To be designated; source Forge records remain “Draft—Head of School Review.”

## Course overview

Students interpret literature, compose narratives, research and explain ideas, and construct and evaluate arguments through four supplied projects. Each student reads, writes, speaks, listens, and keeps identifiable evidence of learning throughout the sequence.

| Course commitments | Plan |
| --- | --- |
| Duration | 16 instructional weeks; 32 × 90-minute blocks; 48 classroom hours |
| Project schedule | Four projects in supplied order; four weeks each; one individual block followed by one group block each week |
| Project work | At least 30 minutes of actual project work in every block, with instruction, feedback, and individual evidence inside the same 90 minutes |
| Calendar boundary | Relative weeks are shown. Dates, holidays, assessment windows, and the purpose of any remaining school-year weeks are unresolved. This document describes the supplied sequence, not a complete annual timetable. |
| Outside work | No required homework is assumed in this draft. Optional rereading/rehearsal is not needed to access the next lesson. |
| Access and evidence | Provide text/tool access and record supports. Group roles do not replace any student's reading, writing, language, or speaking/listening evidence. |

By the end of this sequence, students should be able to support interpretations with text evidence; explain how form and language affect meaning; write and revise narratives, explanations, and arguments for an audience; evaluate and integrate sources; and discuss, present, and analyze ideas responsibly. Component criteria and individual evidence appear in the project teaching documents.

### Reading and workload review — October 2, 2026

The four projects retain Grade 6 analysis, writing, research and communication demands. Product sizes above are local planning estimates. Reading difficulty is a qualitative judgment; no measured Lexile or confirmed independent reading level is claimed. Guided, partner and individual reading routes are specified in each project. Short responses supply visible evidence without becoming extra essays.

Sustained reading means returning to a connected text across several lessons: E1 whole story in W1 and rereading in W2–3; E2 whole story in W1 and adaptation/rereading in W2–3; E3 linked source set across W1–3; E4 source set revisited as positions develop across W1–3. Keep stopping places and comprehension notes. This is a 16-week term sequence; a full-year course needs its remaining weeks and longer-reading progression planned separately.

E3 replaces the repeated Keller selection with a contemporary Meir first-person interview; it is personal nonfiction, not scientific evidence for the Moon question. W1-G now concentrates on sources and guided poetry; W2-G provides the first interview reading. Source links and assigned boundaries are in the project. More contemporary literary voices remain a selection priority when the term becomes a full-year plan; replace equivalent reading rather than adding workload.

## Strand progression

| Strand | E1 → E2 → E3 → E4 |
| --- | --- |
| Reading | Evidence, theme, plot, craft → form/performance and genre comparison → central idea, structure, purpose, sources → integrated formats and argument evaluation, with continued literary analysis |
| Writing | Interpretive explanation and reflection → original narrative and director's note → researched explanation and literary reflection → argument and a distinct informative brief |
| Language / vocabulary | Pronouns, sentences, conventions, context, morphology, references, etymology → sentence effects, figurative language, relationships, nuance → precise domain language and independent inquiry → deliberate choices for reasoning, tone, and audience |
| Speaking / listening | Prepared discussion and media interpretation → rehearsal and performance decisions → source conference and clear explanation → claims, delivery, another speaker's reasoning, and audience adaptation |
| Research / process | Find and explain passages; plan/revise/edit → compare text evidence and revise craft → question, evaluate, quote/paraphrase, cite, and refocus → integrate credible evidence, address opposing claims, and respond to challenge |

Reading range, short and sustained writing, digital production, discussion, feedback, and language practice continue in every project. E1 establishes these routines; one project does not complete an annual proficiency expectation.

### E1 Story Evidence Bureau · Weeks 1–4

**Role and purpose:** Literary investigator; test competing interpretations against passages and character changes. **Lasting understandings:** Interpretations must account for the whole text. New evidence can change a defensible conclusion. An author's choices shape what readers notice and infer.

**Focus Tennessee standards:** 6.RL.KID.1–3; 6.RL.CS.4–6. Language, reading-range, discussion, and writing-process instruction is distributed through the linked lessons.

### E4 Claims on Trial · Weeks 13–16

Fair attention to opposing claims strengthens reasoning. Effective advocates also listen and adapt.

**Focus Tennessee standards:** 6.RI.IKI.7–9; 6.W.TTP.1; 6.SL.CC.3 and 6.SL.PKI.4–6.
