# E2 Voices Across Forms

Grade 6 ELA project teaching document. **Step 5: eight detailed lesson drafts and assessment specifications, October 2, 2026.** Tennessee baseline: 2027–28. Proposed texts and performance resources are identified below. Text/access review, classroom pacing trials, and Step 6 material production remain open. [Open the lesson plans](#step5).

## Supplied project

- **Role:** Adaptation director.
- **Goal:** Explore how sequence, viewpoint, and performance choices change meaning.
- **Final experience:** A live staged reading paired with a text-to-performance comparison.
- **Time:** Four weeks; eight 90-minute lessons, four individual and four group. The lesson-specific budget reserves 720 minutes, including at least 30 minutes of actual project work in every block; practical fit remains conditional on resource and readiness checks.
- **Primary assignments:** FF.G6.ELA.03, FF.G6.ELA.05, FF.G6.ELA.11, FF.G6.ELA.20.
- **Recurring assignments:** FF.G6.ELA.01, FF.G6.ELA.02, FF.G6.ELA.04, FF.G6.ELA.07, FF.G6.ELA.08, FF.G6.ELA.09, FF.G6.ELA.10, FF.G6.ELA.15, FF.G6.ELA.16, FF.G6.ELA.21, FF.G6.ELA.23.

### FF.G6.ELA.03 — Vary sentence patterns and maintain style

**Supplied Forge wording:** Vary sentence patterns for meaning and reader interest while maintaining consistent style and tone.

**Tennessee reference:** 6.L.KL.3 (PDF pp. 23). **Common Core reference:** CCSS.ELA-LITERACY.L.6.3 (PDF pp. 52). **Handoff teaching:** Project instruction. **Later assigned recurrence:** E3, E4.

**Prerequisites for these targets:** ELA.01d sentence forms; a distinction between audience and narrator.

**Likely difficulty and support:** Variety may become random sentence switching; style may change unintentionally. Compare two versions of the same message and make the reader/listener effect explicit.

**Instruction and practice:** Model changes in length, openings, and sentence structure; revise a narrative passage and perform it twice. Return to style and tone in E3 explanation and E4 argument.

### FF.G6.ELA.05 — Interpret figurative language and word relationships

**Supplied Forge wording:** Explain and use figurative language, word relationships, connotation, and denotation in reading, writing, speaking, and listening.

**Tennessee reference:** 6.L.VAU.5 (PDF pp. 25). **Common Core reference:** CCSS.ELA-LITERACY.L.6.5 (PDF pp. 53). **Handoff teaching:** E2-A. **Later assigned recurrence:** E3, E4.

**Prerequisites for these targets:** ELA.04 contextual meaning; literal meaning and word categories.

**Likely difficulty and support:** Literal reading can obscure figurative meaning; similar definitions may suggest false interchangeability. Use paired contexts and spoken examples with contrasting effects.

**Instruction and practice:** Under E2-A model interpretation and intentional use. Sort relationships, compare connotations, revise lines, and explain oral language. Retrieve with new cases in E3-B and E4-B.

### FF.G6.ELA.11 — Compare representations and related texts

**Supplied Forge wording:** Compare the experience of reading a text with audio, video, or live versions and compare texts in different forms or genres.

**Tennessee reference:** 6.RL.IKI.7; 6.RL.IKI.9 (PDF pp. 36, 39). **Common Core reference:** CCSS.ELA-LITERACY.RL.6.7; CCSS.ELA-LITERACY.RL.6.9 (PDF pp. 37). **Handoff teaching:** E2-B. **Later assigned recurrence:** E4.

### FF.G6.ELA.20 — Craft effective narratives

**Supplied Forge wording:** Develop real or imagined experiences using context, sequence, pacing, dialogue, description, precise language, and a reflective conclusion.

**Tennessee reference:** 6.W.TTP.3 (PDF pp. 61). **Common Core reference:** CCSS.ELA-LITERACY.W.6.3 (PDF pp. 43). **Handoff teaching:** Project instruction. **Later assigned recurrence:** None in the supplied map; retain practice and transfer within this project.

**Prerequisites for these targets:** ELA.07–10 literary analysis; ELA.21 writing process; ELA.03/05 style and language.

**Likely difficulty and support:** Adapting an author’s scene can replace original writing; sequencing may omit a smooth progression or reflection. Use planning models and comparison, while keeping the student’s original narrative identifiable.

**Instruction and practice:** Model a narrative opening, sequence, technique, transitions, sensory language, and reflective closure. Practice short passages, compose an individual real/imagined narrative, revise, and use a fresh narrative segment for transfer.

## Step 3 — Success criteria and weekly milestones

**Essential question:** How do form, viewpoint, and performance shape meaning?

**Lasting understandings:** A rendition interprets a written text through choices. Different genres can approach a shared topic in different ways. Narrative and language choices guide a reader's or listener's experience.

### Success criteria for the live staged reading

The audience is classmates and the teacher. Teams deliver a three- to four-minute live staged reading of a scene from the author story. They make deliberate choices about sequence, viewpoint, pacing, voice, and language, and explain those choices with source passages. Every learner contributes live through an accessible communication mode and supplies individual analysis. A production role alone does not establish speaking, reading, or writing evidence.

Keep three individual products identifiable: **an original narrative**, **a text-to-performance comparison**, and **a cross-genre comparison**. The two comparisons can be labeled sections of one director memo for future performers. An adaptation of the supplied story is not the student's original narrative; the script and narrative stay separate. The group performance, acting confidence, costumes, or popularity cannot replace individual learning evidence.

### Eight-block feasibility budget

Minutes do not overlap. **Teach/practice** covers models, guided reading, and supported cases; **project work** covers actual reading analysis, composing, script decisions, rehearsal, and performance. **Feedback** includes targeted reteaching; **checks** includes individual retrieval and new applications; **setup** includes transitions. Evidence also comes from authentic work. Listening to instruction or doing a general worksheet does not count as protected project work.

Use a compact complete original narrative, provisionally **300–450 words**, with a small cast and a manageable event. Word count is a planning envelope, not a scoring rule. The director memo is a concise piece developed across W2–4, with two labeled evidence-based comparisons; notes and a comparison table may supply supporting details. Do not require duplicate essays. The separate fresh narrative is a compact complete event written in 25 minutes, not another polished project.

The W4-I 40-minute continuous interval is for the complete original narrative using the student's own plan/draft. Record actual production conditions. This local allowance is not a mandated typing test duration or a speed grade. Trial reading, writing, access, transitions, and the assessment feature load before treating the budget as classroom-feasible.

Use provisional ensembles of three or four. Paired ensembles perform simultaneously; each reading lasts three to four minutes. Build individual observations through W1–4 instead of assuming one teacher can hear everyone simultaneously at the final event. Confirm enrollment, audibility, access, and review capacity. Cut props, elaborate staging, and redundant rehearsal first if time is short. If required work still cannot fit, record a pacing decision and update the class map rather than add hidden homework or omit a component.

## Step 5 — Detailed lesson drafts

### Student project guide

You are an adaptation director and an original storyteller. Read and compare texts, write your own narrative, and work with a team on a staged reading. Keep your original story and your two kinds of comparison separate. Each lesson below tells you what to do and what to save. The expandable teacher planning reference keeps the original scheduling and standards details separate.

**How the project is graded:** Your teacher will review the project work named in each lesson and tell you which parts are clear and supported, which need revision and which have not yet been shown. Drafts and practice lead to feedback and improvement. Team work does not replace your own writing, reading or speaking/listening evidence. This plan does not set point values, grade weights or letter-grade cutoffs.

### Product size and reading support

Write one complete original story of about 300–450 words, with a small cast, one main event, purposeful details and an ending. Add a director memo with two labeled paragraphs of about 100–150 words each: text compared with performance, and story compared with poem. Perform a simple three- to four-minute team reading using scripts; costumes, memorization and a slide deck are unnecessary.

These are local workload estimates, not standards, grading cutoffs or a typing-speed requirement. Short practice notes receive feedback; the completed main product and named individual reading/speaking evidence receive final review. Reuse notes and drafts. No required homework is added.

Read After Twenty Years across Week 1, then reread the full story while planning its short adaptation in Weeks 2–3. Your teacher will explain unfamiliar idioms and model tracking what the narrator reveals. Read all of The Road Not Taken together before discussing its speaker; compare what the poem says about both roads rather than assuming a ready-made moral. Read the short theatre memoir with vocabulary support.

**Examples to prepare in Step 6:** A short worked example using a different passage, a developing response with one specific improvement, and a complete model matching the product size above. Keep the student’s assessed interpretation or source choice open.

Keep each student's original narrative, two labeled comparison sections, dated short notes, word/language record, reading/access record, and digital contributions in one portfolio. Begin team decisions with individual preparation and end with each learner's own explanation. Models use different examples from independent checks. Retain teacher/peer feedback and an identifiable substantive change; edit conventions separately.

Record the learner, target, task/date, actual evidence, support, and next action. For reading/listening and writing/speaking targets, collect the required modes across scheduled work. ### Descriptive scoring guidance

Use **Demonstrated**, **Developing**, **Not yet demonstrated**, or **Not observed** for each applicable criterion. These are planning feedback labels, not a school grading scale or passing threshold. Developing means some accurate evidence with a named missing part; not yet means an attempted response does not demonstrate the criterion; not observed means the required feature, task, mode, or individual contribution is absent. Record permitted guidance/access separately rather than automatically penalize it.

| Dimension / targets | Demonstrated evidence | Partial evidence and next teaching action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Narrative context and sequence · .20a–b | Original opening establishes context, viewpoint, narrator/participants; events develop naturally and connect smoothly. | A setting label or event list alone is incomplete. Model a connection, then ask for a new opening/event application. |
| Narrative technique/detail/ending · .20c–e | Appropriate dialogue, pacing, description, shift transitions, precise/sensory details, and a relevant reflective conclusion develop the experience. | Technique labels, disconnected details, or abrupt/unrelated closure are incomplete. Address the missing component and check a fresh compact event. |
| Sentence effects/style · .03a–b | Written and spoken choices show purposeful sentence variation and coherent style/tone; learner explains effects. | Random variety or an unexplained change is incomplete. Compare two versions and apply the decision to another passage. |
| Figurative meaning/relationships/nuance · .05a–c | Correct read/heard interpretation, intentional written/spoken use, explained word relationship, and accurate connotation/denotation choices. | A named figure or synonym alone is incomplete. Record the missing mode/meaning and use a different case after modeling. |
| Text-to-performance comparison · .11a | Specific written and performed evidence supports an explanation of imagined versus perceived experience and meaning. | Preference, plot recall, or performance detail without a source link is incomplete. Repeat a short scene and compare a different choice. |
| Cross-genre comparison · .11b | Evidence from both author texts explains their approaches to a shared theme/topic and the role of form/structure/viewpoint. | Separate summaries or a story-versus-its-adaptation response do not establish the criterion. | Process/routines/discussion · .16/.21/.23 | Prepared responsive contributions, meaningful media interpretation, dated writing across purposes, substantive revision/editing, and attributable digital work with production conditions. | Team completion or polished prose alone does not show the process.
