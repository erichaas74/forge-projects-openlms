# S3 — Weather Route Control

Updated 2026-10-03. Steps 1–5 planning drafted and reviewed; materials, access arrangements and classroom timing remain to verify in Step 6. This is a 16-week sequence, not a verified full-year course or student mastery claim.

[Course map](../GRADE_6_SCIENCE_SCOPE_AND_SEQUENCE.md) · [Science workbook](<../../6th Grade/Grade_6_Science_Curriculum_Standards.xlsx>)

## Project overview

**Question:** How can Earth-system models and weather evidence help us revise a route forecast?

**What students do:** Build connected models of air, ocean and water movement; collect weather records and revise a route briefing when new observations arrive.

**Final product:** One route briefing file with a route map, linked system diagrams, weather log, evidence-based forecast and revised recommendation. Short individual models/checks remain attachments, not extra polished reports.

**Lasting understandings:**
- Unequal heating, Earth’s rotation and geography shape circulation and regional climates.
- Water follows multiple pathways driven by solar energy and gravity.
- Weather forecasts use changing observations and uncertainty; climate explanations use longer evidence records.

**Schedule:** Four weeks; eight 90-minute blocks, individual then group each week. No required homework. Minimum 30 minutes of actual project work per block. Class-size assumption: 24 students/six groups of four; confirm before teaching.

**Evidence and access:** Keep individual reasoning and support records separate from team product quality. Use the lesson-specific criteria below; no invented weights or cutoffs. Provide readable diagrams, vocabulary previews, chunked text, captions and equivalent oral/text responses while preserving the scientific task. Physical construction/test expectations still require direct accessible participation. Extend with an alternate explanation or constraint, not an extra polished product.

## Eight lesson plans

### S3-W1-I — Model uneven heating and moving air

**Week 1 / Individual / 90 minutes.**

**Focus and project connection:** Build the first circulation model for the route file.

**Resources:** [NOAA — Weather systems and patterns](https://www.noaa.gov/education/resource-collections/weather-atmosphere/weather-systems-patterns). Global winds selection and latitude diagram; about 180 words. Teacher-created tasks named below are specifications pending Step 6, not finished handouts.

**Instruction and modeling:** Use a globe and light-spread diagram to compare energy per area by latitude. Trace warm less-dense air rising and cooler air replacing it. Explain convection as moving matter; a rising arrow alone is not a circulation loop.

**Activity sequence:**
1. Annotate an uneven-heating diagram with evidence from a light-spread model.
2. Draw a closed atmospheric circulation loop with warming/cooling locations.
3. Compare model predictions to a simple wind/temperature pattern.
4. Attach the model to a fictional route map and identify a question about likely climate.
5. Individually explain why “heat always rises” is an incomplete description.

**Individual work and assessment:** Individual convection diagram with solar source, density changes and return flow.

**Quality criteria:** Connect uneven heating to moving air and energy transfer; identify what the model simplifies.

**Feedback, support and fresh recheck:** If arrows show only upward movement, track replacement air and recheck a new convection situation. Provide labeled locations but leave arrows blank; extension distinguishes radiation from convection.

**Preparation and next handoff:** Prepare accessible globe/diagram demonstration and route map. Leave rotation and full ocean reasoning for W1-G to avoid overload.

**Skills taught and checked:**
Teach / revisit: Build the first circulation model for the route file.  
Practice: Develop an atmospheric circulation component.  
Check: Individual convection diagram with solar source, density changes and return flow.  
Builds on: S1 energy pathways; locate equator and poles.

**Three-dimensional focus:** DCI contributions: ESS2.C/D. Practice: Develop/use models. Crosscutting reasoning: Systems and system models.

**National standards:** Primary: MS-ESS2-6. Supporting: MS-PS3-3. Confirmed in plan for the named contribution; whole performance expectation is evidenced across its mapped route. Materials and observed learning remain separate.

### S3-W1-G — Connect ocean currents and Earth’s rotation

**Week 1 / Group / 90 minutes.**

**Focus and project connection:** Use temperature and salinity evidence to extend the system model.

**Resources:** [NOAA — Thermohaline Circulation](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_currents/05conveyor1.html); [NOAA — Weather systems and patterns](https://www.noaa.gov/education/resource-collections/weather-atmosphere/weather-systems-patterns). NOAA circulation diagram and short rotation caption; about 180 words. Teacher-created tasks named below are specifications pending Step 6, not finished handouts.

**Instruction and modeling:** Model density comparisons with teacher-recorded fresh/salt and warm/cool water observations, varying one factor at a time. Add the effect of Earth’s rotation on broad circulation paths and continental boundaries; no Coriolis calculations.

**Activity sequence:**
1. Interpret temperature/salinity density observations and identify controls.
2. Draw surface/deep ocean movement and explain energy transport.
3. Use a globe/rotating-map representation to annotate deflection and continent constraints.
4. Compare the ocean and atmospheric models and connect them without claiming identical mechanisms.
5. Each student predicts a circulation change and supports it with evidence.

**Individual work and assessment:** Linked ocean/atmosphere model and private salinity/rotation explanation.

**Quality criteria:** Explain density from temperature/salinity, solar energy input and rotation/land boundaries; observations support the claim.

**Feedback, support and fresh recheck:** If salt is described as pulling water downward, compare equal-volume masses and density, then recheck a different pair. Provide a pre-labeled map; extension identifies a model limitation.

**Preparation and next handoff:** Validate demonstration footage/data; do not mix several unobserved variable changes. Bring circulation model to regional-climate work.

**Skills taught and checked:**
Teach / revisit: Use temperature and salinity evidence to extend the system model.  
Practice: Extend and use a circulation model.  
Check: Linked ocean/atmosphere model and private salinity/rotation explanation.  
Builds on: Convection loop and relative density.

**Three-dimensional focus:** DCI contributions: ESS2.C/D. Practice: Develop/use models. Crosscutting reasoning: Systems and system models.

### S3-W2-I — Explain why regions have different climates

**Week 2 / Individual / 90 minutes.**

**Focus and project connection:** Use circulation and geographic features to explain regional patterns.

**Resources:** [NOAA — Weather systems and patterns](https://www.noaa.gov/education/resource-collections/weather-atmosphere/weather-systems-patterns); [NOAA — Thermohaline Circulation](https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/education/tutorial_currents/05conveyor1.html). Three concise regional profiles and a mountain cross-section; about 200 words. Teacher-created tasks named below are specifications pending Step 6, not finished handouts.

**Instruction and modeling:** Model two locations at similar latitude but different ocean-current exposure. Add altitude, coast/interior and mountain lifting/rain-shadow reasoning. Distinguish a long-term climate pattern from today’s weather.

**Activity sequence:**
1. Compare regional temperature/precipitation profiles using axes and reference periods.
2. Trace atmospheric/ocean heat transfer on the route map.
3. Annotate a mountain rain-shadow diagram with rising/cooling/condensing and descending/drier air.
4. Explain how at least two geographic/circulation factors affect one route region.
5. Individually predict a contrasting region and name uncertainty.

**Individual work and assessment:** Individual regional-climate explanation and revised system map.

**Quality criteria:** Use model mechanisms and data; include ocean and atmosphere connections and geographic variation without treating latitude as the only cause.

**Feedback, support and fresh recheck:** If one rainy day is called climate, contrast daily and long-term records and recheck another statement. Offer paired profile frames; extension examines two plausible influences.

**Preparation and next handoff:** Provide dated, comparable climate summaries; preserve annotations for the final briefing rather than a separate geography report.

**Skills taught and checked:**
Teach / revisit: Use circulation and geographic features to explain regional patterns.  
Practice: Use a model to explain regional patterns.  
Check: Individual regional-climate explanation and revised system map.  
Builds on: Atmosphere/ocean model, axes and location.

**Three-dimensional focus:** DCI contributions: ESS2.C/D. Practice: Develop/use models. Crosscutting reasoning: Systems and system models.

### S3-W2-G — Follow water and explain landscape changes

**Week 2 / Group / 90 minutes.**

**Focus and project connection:** Model water pathways and examine human and organism impacts.

**Resources:** [USGS — The Water Cycle](https://labs.waterdata.usgs.gov/visualizations/water-cycle/index.html). USGS diagram and two small comparison datasets; about 150 new words. Teacher-created tasks named below are specifications pending Step 6, not finished handouts.

**Instruction and modeling:** Model two paths from ocean to atmosphere to land and back, labeling state changes, Sun-driven processes and gravity. Use vegetation removal/regrowth evidence and organism effects such as transpiration/root stabilization; distinguish landform influence on water from changes organisms cause to land.

**Activity sequence:**
1. Trace multiple water pathways including groundwater and transpiration.
2. Compare infiltration/runoff/erosion evidence for vegetated and cleared sites.
3. Interpret an organism-impact case linking root or burrowing changes to land and a vegetation/transpiration record to atmospheric moisture.
4. Revise the route water model with a supported human and nonhuman effect.
5. Individually explain a fresh pathway and one evidence limit.

**Individual work and assessment:** Individual water-cycle model plus two short data-backed impact explanations covering human and other-organism effects.

**Quality criteria:** Separate energy driver from gravity; conserve water through pathways; connect evidence to water, landform and atmosphere effects without overclaiming.

**Feedback, support and fresh recheck:** If the cycle is a single fixed loop, give a groundwater starting point and recheck two return paths. If organism effects are omitted, trace plant-water-air transfers before a fresh check.

**Preparation and next handoff:** Prepare validated short datasets; avoid reading a long article here. Carry unfinished data explanation into W3-I opening; retain a 35-minute model-work window.

**Skills taught and checked:**
Teach / revisit: Model water pathways and examine human and organism impacts.  
Practice: Trace and revise a coupled water-system model.  
Check: Individual water-cycle model plus two short data-backed impact explanations covering human and other-organism effects.  
Builds on: States of water, Sun/energy, regional climate.

**Three-dimensional focus:** DCI contributions: ESS2.C. Practice: Develop models and analyze data. Crosscutting reasoning: Energy and matter.

### S3-W3-I — Explain greenhouse warming

**Week 3 / Individual / 90 minutes.**

**Focus and project connection:** Model energy entering, leaving and interacting with greenhouse gases.

**Resources:** [NASA — Causes of climate change](https://science.nasa.gov/climate-change/causes/). Simplified NASA radiation graphic and about 180 words. Teacher-created tasks named below are specifications pending Step 6, not finished handouts.

**Instruction and modeling:** Draw incoming sunlight, surface absorption and outgoing infrared radiation. Explain greenhouse gases absorbing/emitting infrared energy and habitability; avoid ozone-hole explanations or a sealed-jar analogy as proof of atmospheric radiation.

**Activity sequence:**
1. Repair a water-model issue in the opening clinic.
2. Build an Earth energy model with an explicit atmosphere boundary.
3. Compare natural greenhouse warming with an increase in greenhouse gases.
4. Use a supplied dated graph to frame a question about long-term warming evidence.
5. Individually explain the mechanism and why one cold day does not disprove a global trend.

**Individual work and assessment:** Individual greenhouse model and an evidence-clarifying question for the climate investigation.

**Quality criteria:** Distinguish incoming/outgoing radiation, natural/enhanced effects and weather/climate timescales; question names evidence needed.

**Feedback, support and fresh recheck:** If gases are said to create energy or block all sunlight, trace sources and infrared pathways; recheck a corrected model. Provide radiation labels; extend by asking about energy balance over time.

**Preparation and next handoff:** Prepare accessible graphics and verified time-series labels; save broader attribution comparison for W4-I.

**Skills taught and checked:**
Teach / revisit: Model energy entering, leaving and interacting with greenhouse gases.  
Practice: Construct a radiation model and ask a question.  
Check: Individual greenhouse model and an evidence-clarifying question for the climate investigation.  
Builds on: Energy conservation and regional climate versus weather.

**Three-dimensional focus:** DCI contributions: ESS3.D. Practice: Ask questions and develop models. Crosscutting reasoning: Stability and change.

### S3-W3-G — Collect weather evidence for a forecast

**Week 3 / Group / 90 minutes.**

**Focus and project connection:** Use changing observations to explain air-mass interactions.

**Resources:** [NOAA — Weather systems and patterns](https://www.noaa.gov/education/resource-collections/weather-atmosphere/weather-systems-patterns). Air masses/fronts selections plus three station/map snapshots; about 180 words. Teacher-created tasks named below are specifications pending Step 6, not finished handouts.

**Instruction and modeling:** Model extracting temperature, pressure, humidity, wind and precipitation at a fixed station across times. Trace front movement and explain why a forecast states likelihood and uncertainty rather than certainty.

**Activity sequence:**
1. Collect observations from a source-labeled archived weather sequence in a shared log.
2. Compare the route’s stations and locate contrasting air masses/fronts.
3. Connect changes in the measurements to the passage of a front.
4. Draft a probable weather forecast and route recommendation.
5. Each student explains two observations and one uncertainty before the team combines ideas.

**Individual work and assessment:** Individual data-log entries and an observation-based probabilistic forecast.

**Quality criteria:** Use multiple weather variables across time; link evidence to interacting air masses; distinguish measurement from forecast.

**Feedback, support and fresh recheck:** If a symbol alone is treated as proof of rain, compare humidity/pressure/wind evidence and recheck a new snapshot. Provide a symbol key; extension considers forecast confidence.

**Preparation and next handoff:** Select archived maps with timestamps/timezone, units and source; prepare final fresh overlay separately. No real-world travel or emergency advice.

**Skills taught and checked:**
Teach / revisit: Use changing observations to explain air-mass interactions.  
Practice: Collect and interpret a time sequence.  
Check: Individual data-log entries and an observation-based probabilistic forecast.  
Builds on: Air circulation, water processes and graph reading.

**Three-dimensional focus:** DCI contributions: ESS2.C/D. Practice: Plan investigations and collect data. Crosscutting reasoning: Cause and effect.

### S3-W4-I — Question the evidence for climate change

**Week 4 / Individual / 90 minutes.**

**Focus and project connection:** Compare long-term evidence and finish the connected briefing.

**Resources:** [NASA — Causes of climate change](https://science.nasa.gov/climate-change/causes/). Dated temperature, greenhouse-gas, solar and volcanic evidence panels; about 150 words. Teacher-created tasks named below are specifications pending Step 6, not finished handouts.

**Instruction and modeling:** Model a question that clarifies a timescale, measurement or proposed cause, rather than a yes/no opinion. Compare human greenhouse-gas forcing and natural factors; explain that several independent records support the dominant recent human contribution.

**Activity sequence:**
1. Use an opening clinic on the weakest system-model component.
2. Annotate a global-temperature trend and relevant human/natural factor records.
3. Write two answerable questions that would clarify evidence or distinguish explanations.
4. Use the packet to address one question and acknowledge a remaining limit.
5. Complete the route briefing’s system panels without turning climate attribution into a day-to-day forecast.

**Individual work and assessment:** Individual evidence annotations, two precise questions and a source-based response; completed model attachments.

**Quality criteria:** Questions identify variables/timescales and clarify causal evidence; distinguish global climate change from local weather predictions.

**Feedback, support and fresh recheck:** If questions only ask opinions, identify a measurable variable and rewrite; recheck a new evidence panel. Offer question stems without supplying the answer; extension compares timescales.

**Preparation and next handoff:** Verify graph dates, baselines and source attribution; prepare short individual model prompts for unresolved water/greenhouse components.

**Skills taught and checked:**
Teach / revisit: Compare long-term evidence and finish the connected briefing.  
Practice: Ask and refine evidence questions.  
Check: Individual evidence annotations, two precise questions and a source-based response; completed model attachments.

### S3-W4-G — Brief and revise the route forecast

**Week 4 / Group / 90 minutes.**

**Focus and project connection:** Use a new observation to revise the forecast and explain mechanisms.

**Resources:** [NOAA — Weather systems and patterns](https://www.noaa.gov/education/resource-collections/weather-atmosphere/weather-systems-patterns). Final route file and held-back weather observation; no new article. Teacher-created tasks named below are specifications pending Step 6, not finished handouts.

**Instruction and modeling:** Model a short briefing structure: conditions, evidence, probable development, recommendation, uncertainty. Demonstrate marking what changed rather than starting a second report.

**Activity sequence:**
1. Teams present the forecast with circulation and geographic evidence.
2. Each member explains an observation or system connection.
3. Release a new front/pressure snapshot; teams revise the same route file.
4. Individually justify a forecast revision and complete a targeted fresh model question.
5. Archive the weather log, models and unresolved needs for S4 environmental monitoring.

**Individual work and assessment:** Individual revised forecast, targeted model response and teacher-recorded explanation; team briefing judged separately.

**Quality criteria:** New evidence changes or supports the recommendation for an explained reason; account for uncertainty and preserve causal model logic.

**Feedback, support and fresh recheck:** If the team ignores contrary evidence, compare original/new measurements and request one warranted revision. Recheck individually rather than counting a spokesperson’s answer for all.

**Preparation and next handoff:** Six teams of four is a pacing assumption; use 30 minutes for 5-minute briefings and short private checks afterward. Increase the observation plan if enrollment differs.
